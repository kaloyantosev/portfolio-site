## 3. The Physical Chokepoint: The Strait of Hormuz and Qatari LNG

To understand why Dutch TTF natural gas is the primary beneficiary of a U.S.-Iran escalation, one must examine the physical geography of global energy transit. While commentators routinely focus on oil tankers passing through the Persian Gulf, the liquefied natural gas (LNG) dimension is far more critical and far less resilient.

- Geographic Exposure: Iran borders the entire northern boundary of the Strait of Hormuz, the world's most critical maritime energy bottleneck. At its narrowest point, the shipping lanes are only two nautical miles wide in each direction.
- The Qatari Vulnerability: Qatar is one of the top three LNG exporters on Earth (alongside the United States and Australia), supplying approximately 20% of global seaborne LNG. Crucially, 100% of Qatar's LNG export fleet must transit through the Strait of Hormuz to reach international buyers. Qatar has no overland bypass pipelines for LNG.

In the event of kinetic military escalation between the U.S. and Iran, Iran does not even need to physically close the Strait with naval blockades. The mere threat of drone strikes, ballistic missile launches, or naval mining causes maritime insurance syndicates (such as Lloyd's of London Joint War Committee) to cancel war-risk coverage or increase insurance premiums by hundreds of percent. Commercial tanker operators will halt voyages through the Strait. A shutdown of Hormuz removes one-fifth of the entire planet's LNG supply overnight. Unlike oil, which can be drawn from global strategic petroleum reserves (SPR), there is no equivalent global strategic reserve for natural gas.

## 4. Europe's Fragile Post-2022 Energy Architecture

Why does a disruption in the Persian Gulf hit Europe harder than any other region? The answer lies in the dramatic structural transformation of the European energy market following the 2022 Russia-Ukraine war.

Prior to 2022, Europe relied on steady, low-cost Russian pipeline gas (via Nord Stream, Yamal-Europe, and Druzhba) for over 40% of its consumption. Following the destruction of Nord Stream and political decoupling, Europe replaced pipeline supply by building floating storage and regasification units (FSRUs) and importing massive volumes of seaborne LNG. Today, LNG accounts for nearly 40% to 50% of Europe's total gas imports.

This shift fundamentally transformed Europe from a captive pipeline market with long-term contracts into a spot-reliant buyer competing in the global merchant shipping arena. Europe possesses virtually no domestic production cushion; its fields in the North Sea and the Netherlands (Groningen) are either depleted or officially decommissioned. Consequently, any global supply contraction immediately translates into acute pricing pressure at the Dutch TTF hub.

## 5. The Intercontinental Arbitrage War: Europe vs. Asia

A common misconception is that because Europe imports substantial LNG from the United States, a disruption in Middle Eastern supply will not affect European supplies. This completely ignores the mechanics of global energy trade.

Roughly 70% to 80% of Qatari LNG currently flows eastward to Asian buyers — primarily China, Japan, South Korea, India, and Taiwan. These economies depend heavily on imported LNG for base-load power generation and industrial heating.

If Qatari LNG shipments through the Strait of Hormuz are delayed, halted, or rerouted, Asian utilities will not simply turn off their electric grids. Instead, Asian buyers will instantly enter the global spot market to secure replacement cargoes from the Atlantic Basin — principally the United States, West Africa, and Norway. Because US LNG contracts are destination-flexible, cargoes flow to whichever market pays the highest price.

This triggers an aggressive international bidding war. For Europe to keep its regasification terminals supplied, Dutch TTF prices must surge to a premium over the Asian benchmark (JKM — Japan Korea Marker) to literally buy tankers away from Asia. European gas prices cannot remain isolated; they must spike high enough to cause demand destruction across European industry and outbid desperate Asian utilities.

## 6. Maritime Shipping Bottlenecks and Transit Deficits

Even before a direct U.S.-Iran conflict, global maritime logistics were already operating under severe strain due to Houthi attacks in the Red Sea, which forced LNG tankers to avoid the Bab el-Mandeb Strait and the Suez Canal. Vessels traveling between the Middle East and Europe were already forced to navigate around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10 to 14 days per voyage.

This extended routing ties up specialized LNG vessels for significantly longer durations, creating an artificial shortage of global carrier capacity. If the Strait of Hormuz is compromised, the remaining global fleet must travel longer distances from alternative production basins to meet Asian and European demand simultaneously. The compounding effect of tanker shortages and surging charter rates directly inflates delivered gas prices at European import terminals, providing further upward momentum to TTF futures.

## 7. Storage Complacency: The Summer Refill Dilemma

Throughout early 2026, financial headlines highlighted European gas storage levels, creating a false sense of security that Europe was 'well-supplied.' This perception reflects dangerous market complacency.

European underground gas storage facilities are designed as seasonal buffers, not permanent replacements for ongoing supply. Storage can supply only about 25% to 30% of Europe's total annual demand. More importantly, as winter withdrawals draw storage down, Europe enters the critical April-to-October injection season, during which utilities are legally mandated by the European Union to refill storage facilities to at least 90% capacity ahead of the following winter.

If Middle Eastern supply disruptions occur just as Europe must begin competing for summer replenishment volumes, European utilities will be forced to buy natural gas aggressively at any price. What the market currently views as adequate inventory can rapidly turn into an acute structural deficit within weeks of disrupted import flows.
