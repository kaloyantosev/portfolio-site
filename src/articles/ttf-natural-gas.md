## 1. Summary

When a serious geopolitical crisis breaks out – especially one involving the United States and Iran – the usual reaction is to buy gold and silver before markets open on Monday.

I think that reaction is worth questioning this time. My earlier analysis of gold suggested it had reached a cycle top: three regression channels (28-year, 5-year and 1-year) all topped out between $5,400 and $5,600, with record leverage and very bullish sentiment on top. Buying gold and silver after that run, as a hedge against a new shock, looks less attractive than it seems.

The exposure I think the market is missing is European natural gas, priced at the Dutch TTF hub. Attention has been on precious metals and crude oil, but Europe is still very exposed to a gas supply shock, and prices do not seem to reflect it. This report expands on the thread I posted about it.

## 2. The Thread

Ahead of the trading week, I posted this on Threads (@ktsvv_):

> "Buying gold and silver right now is not the best option to hedge against the U.S. - Iran war. Both have been on a remarkable run and are attracting investors to buy in on Monday open. However, the analysis I have conducted says otherwise. We are in for an energy crisis that the markets did not fully price in. A thread on why I looked elsewhere for a hedge:"

The point was simple. While many people were getting ready to buy metals that had already run a long way, the more direct risk from a U.S.-Iran conflict is to the global supply of liquefied natural gas (LNG), and Europe is the buyer most exposed to it.

## 3. The Strait of Hormuz and Qatari LNG

The case for TTF starts with geography. Commentary tends to focus on oil tankers in the Persian Gulf, but LNG is the more fragile part of the picture.

- Location: Iran runs along the whole northern side of the Strait of Hormuz. At its narrowest, each shipping lane is about two nautical miles wide.
- Qatar's dependence: Qatar is one of the three largest LNG exporters, alongside the United States and Australia, and supplies roughly 20% of seaborne LNG. All of its LNG exports pass through Hormuz, and there is no pipeline route around it.

Iran would not need to close the strait outright. The threat of drone or missile strikes, or of mines, is enough for war-risk insurers such as the Lloyd's Joint War Committee to withdraw cover or raise premiums sharply, and shipowners would then stop sending tankers through. That would take about a fifth of global LNG supply offline almost at once. Unlike oil, there are no strategic reserves of gas to release.

## 4. Europe After 2022

Europe is more exposed than other regions because of how its gas market changed after Russia invaded Ukraine.

Before 2022, Russian pipeline gas (via Nord Stream, Yamal-Europe and Druzhba) covered over 40% of European consumption. After Nord Stream was damaged and Europe cut ties with Russian supply, it replaced that gas mainly with seaborne LNG, adding floating storage and regasification units (FSRUs) to receive it. LNG is now around 40% to 50% of Europe's gas imports.

That turned Europe from a buyer with long-term pipeline contracts into one that relies on the spot LNG market. Domestic production offers little cushion: North Sea fields are in decline and Groningen in the Netherlands has been shut down. Any drop in global supply therefore shows up quickly in TTF prices.

## 5. Competition with Asia

It is often said that Europe is covered because it buys a lot of American LNG. That overlooks how the market works.

Around 70% to 80% of Qatar's LNG goes to Asia – mainly China, Japan, South Korea, India and Taiwan – where it is used for power generation and industry.

If Qatari cargoes stop, Asian buyers will not go without. They will look for replacement cargoes from the Atlantic Basin, mainly the United States, West Africa and Norway. Most US LNG contracts let cargoes go wherever the price is highest.

That sets up a bidding contest. To keep its import terminals supplied, Europe would need TTF to trade above the Asian benchmark (JKM, the Japan Korea Marker) so that cargoes come to Europe rather than Asia. Prices would have to rise far enough to cut industrial demand in Europe and outbid Asian utilities.

## 6. Shipping

Shipping was already stretched before any direct conflict. Houthi attacks in the Red Sea pushed LNG tankers away from Bab el-Mandeb and the Suez Canal, so voyages between the Middle East and Europe went around the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10 to 14 days.

Longer routes keep each tanker busy for longer, which reduces effective fleet capacity. If Hormuz were disrupted as well, the remaining ships would have to cover longer distances from other supply regions to serve both Asia and Europe. Fewer available ships and higher charter rates both raise the delivered price of gas in Europe.

## 7. Storage

In early 2026, high European storage levels led many to treat the region as well supplied. I think that reading is too relaxed.

Storage is a seasonal buffer rather than a substitute for supply. It covers only about 25% to 30% of annual demand. After winter draws it down, the EU requires storage to be refilled to at least 90% between April and October.

If a Middle East disruption hits just as that refilling season begins, European utilities will have to buy whatever it costs. What looks like comfortable storage today could turn into a shortfall within a few weeks of lost imports.

## 8. Comparing the Two Trades

The main argument of the thread is about risk and reward. Comparing gold with Dutch TTF:

1. Gold and silver: after reaching the meeting point of three regression channels ($5,400–$5,600), gold was about two standard deviations above its 28-year trend, with record leverage and near-universal bullishness. The upside looked limited and the risk of a forced sell-off was high.
2. Dutch TTF: gas had spent months near its post-crisis lows, largely ignored by speculators and pricing in little geopolitical risk. The downside looked limited and the upside in an energy crunch was large.

In short, I would rather own the asset whose supply chain sits directly in the path of the crisis but whose price has not moved yet, than the one everyone is already buying at record highs. For me, that is European natural gas.

## 9. Conclusion and What to Watch

Calling the gold top at $5,400 on January 29 was the first part of this view. The second is the expectation that markets will come to see a U.S.-Iran conflict mainly as an energy supply problem, with Europe most affected.

As I wrote in the thread, buying gold and silver on Monday means buying into stretched prices, heavy leverage and crowded sentiment. I expect the effect to show up instead in Dutch TTF, as Qatari shipping risk, Europe's reliance on LNG and competition with Asia become clearer.

What I am watching:

- Shipping through Hormuz: vessel tracking in the strait and any changes from war-risk insurers.
- The TTF–JKM spread: if Asian prices rise further above Europe's, Atlantic cargoes will head to Asia and Europe will have to respond.
- European forward curves: summer 2026 contracts trading above spot would signal urgent buying for storage.

I may be wrong on timing, but I think the setup favours energy over metals as a hedge for this particular risk.
